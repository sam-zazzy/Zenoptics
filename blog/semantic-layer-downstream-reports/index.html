Define revenue once in a semantic model, expose it to every consumer, and the number stops drifting between tools. That argument is correct. It is also narrower than it sounds, because the model is not the only place your business reads numbers from.

Most enterprises run reports and dashboards that were built before the semantic model existed, or alongside it, or against source systems that never passed through it. A governed model does not reach those. When an AI agent is pointed at the estate rather than at the model, it reaches both.

What a Governed Semantic Model Actually Enforces

This section is the concession, and it deserves the space, because the semantic layer case is stronger than its critics usually allow.

A semantic model does not just hold definitions. Databricks describes governance that travels with the metric itself. Its account names "row-level security, column masking, and certification policies" that travel with each metric definition. They are not re-implemented in every tool. The same account describes policies "inherited by every consuming surface rather than recreated tool by tool."

That is real access control, not documentation. A user who should see one region's figures sees one region's figures, whether the query arrives from a notebook, a dashboard, or an agent. The enforcement happens once, at the model, instead of being rebuilt per tool and drifting between them.

Cube makes the agent case directly and makes it well. Its position is that "governance should be enforced before SQL is emitted, not after". The agent selects from a governed set instead of authoring SQL against raw tables. For questions the model covers, that is the right architecture.

Gartner places the same bet on a longer horizon. Distinguished VP Analyst Rita Sallam is cited in its March 11, 2026 data and analytics predictions. "By 2030, universal semantic layers will be treated as critical infrastructure, alongside data platforms and cybersecurity."

Infrastructure is the right word. It is also a word that describes a foundation rather than a finished building.

Where the Model's Reach Ends

Governance that travels with a metric definition reaches every consumer of that definition. It does not reach an asset that never consumed it.

Three kinds of asset sit in that gap in most enterprises.

Reports built before the model. Analytics estates accumulate over a decade. A semantic model introduced in year seven governs what is rebuilt on top of it, not the six years of reporting that preceded it.

Reports built beside the model. A business unit with its own analyst, its own extract, and a deadline does not always route through a central model. The output still lands in the same portal and still gets read as a company number.

Reports from systems the model does not cover. Finance, HR and operational platforms often ship their own reporting. Those outputs are authoritative inside their system and invisible to a model that was never connected to them.

None of these are failures of the semantic layer. They sit outside its scope by construction.

The practical consequence is uncomfortable. An enterprise can hold a fully governed model and still carry duplicate reports across several BI platforms that disagree with each other and with the model. The governed number is correct and the ungoverned ones are still reachable.

Extending the model to cover everything is the obvious answer and the slow one. Each asset brought in needs a source mapping, an owner, and a definition somebody will defend. That work is measured in quarters. Agents are being connected in weeks, which is why the gap matters now rather than later. The cost of running several BI platforms is partly the cost of this reconciliation never being finished.

The Two Questions That Fall Outside the Model

Two questions come up constantly in a multi-platform estate, and a semantic layer answers neither of them. This is the core of the semantic layer report governance limits problem.

Which of these dashboards is the one to use? A semantic model can tell you what revenue means. It cannot tell you which dashboard containing a revenue figure is the certified quarterly view, which are team variants, and which should have been retired two years ago. Certification at the asset level is a different record from definition at the model level. This is what a semantic layer does not govern.

Who owns the definition when two assets disagree? The model has an owner. So does each report. When a report built outside the model produces a different number, somebody has to decide which one the business stands behind. That decision is recorded against the assets rather than against the model.

Governed model versus governed estate is not a competition between the two. It is a statement that they are different scopes and both need maintaining.

Why Agents Make This Sharper Than People Did

This gap existed before AI agents and was survivable, because people carried the missing context in their heads.

An analyst who has sat in finance for three years knows the close runs on one specific dashboard. They never look up its certification status, because they already know. The ungoverned assets around it are visible to them and simply ignored.

An agent has no such memory. It evaluates what it can reach on the evidence the estate makes explicit. Point it at a governed model and it behaves well. Point it at the reporting estate, which is what most deployments actually do, and it treats a certified quarterly dashboard and an abandoned team copy as equally available.

The failure is quiet, which is what makes it expensive. An agent reading an uncertified asset does not report an error. It returns a confident number from a source nobody has stood behind since the person who built it changed teams.

The semantic layer AI agents enterprise argument holds for the first case. It was never designed for the second.

Governing the Layer Above the Model

Semantic layer versus report certification is a scope distinction with a practical answer. The two records do different jobs and an enterprise running agents needs both.

Atlas is the analytics system of record. It catalogs analytics assets across BI platforms and holds certification status, ownership, lineage and usage for each one, including the assets that never passed through a semantic model. That is dashboard governance across BI tools, maintained above the platforms rather than inside any one of them. A cross-tool inventory is where it starts, because nothing can be certified until it is known to exist.

Nexus builds business context on that governed record, so an agent can interpret what an asset means and which assets are approved for a given domain. Where a semantic model already supplies the definition, that definition is what the asset should be certified against rather than a second one invented alongside it.

Neither replaces a semantic layer. Where a governed model exists, it remains the better answer for the questions it covers, and its definitions should be the ones the estate certifies against. ZenOptics governs the layer the model does not reach. The difference between a semantic layer and an analytics context layer sets out how the two fit together.

What to Check in Your Own Estate

Start by measuring the gap rather than assuming it. Count the analytics assets an agent can currently reach, then count how many of those resolve through your semantic model. The difference is the ungoverned surface.

Then check whether the assets outside the model carry a certification status at all, and whether that status means the same thing in every platform it came from. Where it does not, record which signal wins.

Then decide whether to extend the model, certify the asset, or retire it. All three are valid outcomes and they carry different costs. Extending the model is the most durable and the slowest. Certifying the asset where it sits is faster and leaves the asset in the platform that owns it. Retiring it is cheapest when nobody has opened it in a year.

Leaving the asset reachable and unlabeled is the only option that is not valid, because that is the state an agent cannot reason about.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are you arguing against semantic layers?

No. For the questions a semantic model covers, enforcing governance before SQL is emitted is the right architecture. The access controls that travel with a metric definition are real enforcement. The argument is about scope, not merit.

Can we just put everything through the semantic model?

Over time, in part. In practice most enterprises carry reporting from systems the model does not cover and assets built before it existed. Extending the model is a multi-year program, and agents are being connected now.

Does this mean our semantic layer investment was wrong?

No. It means it solves the model layer and not the asset layer. The two are complementary, and certified metrics remain the stronger grounding for anything the model does cover.

How do we know which assets fall outside the model?

Inventory what the agent can reach across every connected platform, then trace which of those assets resolve through the model. The remainder is the set that needs certification at the asset level.

Is this the same as a metrics store?

No. A metrics store holds definitions. The question here is different. It asks which of the many reports and dashboards containing a metric the business has approved for a given purpose. That is recorded against the asset rather than the definition.

Where does this leave AI agent deployments?

Scope the agent to assets that have been reviewed for its domain, whether those come from the model or from the wider estate. Authoritative analytics for AI agents covers how that scoping works in practice.

Govern the Model and the Estate

A governed semantic model is worth building. Gartner expects it to be treated as infrastructure by 2030. The governance that travels with a metric definition is the strongest answer available for the questions the model covers.

It is not the whole of analytics governance, and an agent connected to your reporting estate will discover the difference before you do.
